[[Basic Definitions of representation]]

For a prime number $p$, we divide $x \in G$ into parts that is a $p$-element (has order a power of $p$), and a $p'$-element part (has order prime to $p$). Written formally, let $x=x_{u}x_{r}=x_{r}x_{u}$, with $\lvert x_{u} \rvert=p^{\alpha}$ for some $\alpha \in \mathbb{N}$, and $gcd(\lvert x_{r} \rvert,p)=1$. Moreover, a group $H$ is said to be $p$-elementary if it is the direct product of a cyclic group of order prime to $p$ with a $p$-group $P$. Such a group is nilpotent and unique. In particular, let $x$ be a $p'$-element, if $P$ is a Sylow $p$-subgroup of $Z(x)$, then we say the group $H=\langle x \rangle\cdot P$ is **asscoiated** with $x$. 

>[!note] Brauer's Theorem
>Let $G$ be a finite  group and let $V_{p}$ be the subgroup of $R(G)$ generated by characters induced from those of $p$-elementary subgroups of $G$. Then the index of $V_{p}$ in $R(G)$ is finite and prime to $p$.

Let $X(p)$ be the family of $p$-elementary subgroups of $G$. The group $V_{p}$ is the image of the homomorphism $Ind:\bigoplus_{H\in X(p)}R(H)\rightarrow R(G)$. Defined by the induction homomorphisms $Ind_{H}^{G}$, $H\in X(p)$. Then $V_{p}$ is an ideal of $R(G)$, and to proof the prove the theorem it is enough to show that there exists an integer $m$, prime to $p$, such that $m\in V_{p}$. In factm we prove the following more precise result.

> [!note] Another version
> Let $g=p^{n}l$ be the order of $G$, with $gcd(p,l)=1$. Then $l\in V_{p}$. 

*Proof*: 
We first guess what we should do. First we notice that $\mathbb{Z}$ is far to rigid, as $\chi$ may not have values in $\mathbb{Z}$. It is not so difficule to handle this, simply change the base ring from $\mathbb{Z}$ to  $A=\mathbb{Z}[\zeta]$ where $\zeta$ is the n-th root of unity, and with $\mathbb{Q}\cap A=\mathbb{Z}$, we are perfactly sure that this will work. In fact,  consider the tensoring mentioned in [[Artin's theorem]] together with $A$ is a finitly generated $\mathbb{Z}$ module, we know that $(A\otimes V_{p})\cap R(G)=V_{p}$. 

Now we proceed with proofing that $l$ belongs to $V_{p}$, i.e. $l$ is of the form $\sum_{H}a_{H}Ind_{H}^{G}(f_{H})$, with $a_{H}\in A$ and $f_{H}\in R(H)$. How do we proof it? One recall the [[Artin's theorem]]: $g=\sum_{C}Ind_{C}^{G}(\theta_{C})$, then for a general character $\chi$ with integer value, $f=g\chi=\sum_{C} Ind_{C}^{G}(\theta_{C})\chi=\sum_{C}Ind_{C}^{G}(\theta_{C}\cdot Res_{C}^{G}\chi)$, so we are pretty sure that if $p^{n}|\chi$, then $\chi \in V_{p}$. This helps us a lot in construction, as we only need a $\chi\equiv l\pmod{p^{n}}$, in fact, we onlu need a $\chi\equiv1\pmod{p^{n}}$, as $A\otimes V_{p}$ is a ideal. But Eula's theorem tells us that finding a $\chi\equiv1\pmod{p^{n}}$ is more or less equivalent to finding a $\psi \in V_{p}$ such that $\psi(g)\not\equiv0\pmod{p}$ (by taking $\chi=\psi ^{\phi(p^{n})}$). 

First one notice that $\chi(x)\pmod{p}$ depends only on the part $x_{r}$, as $\chi(x)\equiv\chi(x)^{p^{N}}=\chi(x^{p^{N}})=\chi(x_{r})^{p^{N}}\equiv \chi(x_{r})\pmod{p}$ for $\chi$ one dimensional. But since artin's theorem tells us that we only have to work with the case of $G$ cyclic, so the proof will work. Now we proof the following lemma:

**Lemma**: Let $x$ be a $p'$-element of $G$, and let $H$ be a $p$-elementary subgroup of $G$  asscoiated with $x$. Then there exists a function $\psi \in A\otimes R(H)$, with integer values, such that the induced function $\phi'=Ind_{H}^{G}\psi$ has the following properties:
1. $\psi'(x)\not\equiv0\pmod{p}$.
2. $\psi'(s)=0$ for each $p'$-element of $G$ which is not conjugate to $x$.

Let $C$ be the cyclic subgroup of $G$ generated by $x$, and let $Z(x)$ be the centralizer of $x$ in $G$. We have $H=C\times P$, where $P$ is a Sylow-subgroup of $Z(x)$. Let $c$ be the order of $C$, and  let $p^{a}$ be the order of $P$. Let $\psi_{C}$ be the function defined on $C$ by $\psi_{C}(x)=c$ and $\psi_{C}(y)=0$ if $y\neq x$, then we have $\chi_{C}=\sum_{\chi}\chi(x^{-1})\chi$, where $\chi$ runs through the set of irrducible characters  of $C$, then we know that $\phi_{C}\in A\otimes R(C)$. Let $\phi$ be the function on $H=C\times P$ defined by $\phi(xy)=\phi_{C}(x)$ for $x \in C$ and $y\in P$. This is the inverse image of $\psi_{C}$ under the projection $H\rightarrow C$. So we have $\psi \in A\otimes R(H)$. We show now that $\psi$ satisfies the conditions of the lemma: If $s$ is a $p'$-element of $G$ and if $y\in G$, $ysy^{-1}$ is a $p'$-element; if $ysy^{-1}$ belongs to $H$ then it belongs to $C$, and we  have $\psi(ysy^{-1})=0$ whenever $ysy^{-1}\neq x$. It follows that $\psi'(s)=0$ if $s$ is not conjugate to $x$, which proves 2. Moreover, 
$$\psi'(x)=\frac{1}{c\cdot p^{a}}\sum_{yxy^{-1}=x}\psi(x)= \frac{1}{p^{a}}\sum_{yxy^{-1}=x}1=\frac{\#Z(x)}{p^{a}}$$
whence $\psi'(x)\not\equiv0\pmod{p}$ since $p^{a}=\#P$ is the largest power of $p$ dividing $\#Z(x)$. 

Now put $\psi=\sum_{i} \psi_{i}$ where $i$ runs through all representatives of $p$-regular classes (those consisting of $p'$-elements) and $\psi_{i}$ is the corresponding  character in the lemma. Then we are able to find the character $\psi$ we want ($\psi(g)\not\equiv0\pmod{p}$ for all $g \in G$). $\square$

---
From this theorem, we know that: 

**Theorem**: Each character of $G$ is a linear combination with integer coefficients of characters induced from characters of elementary subgroups. 

**Theorem**s: Each character of $G$ is a linear combination with integer coefficients of monomial characters.

**Theorem**: Let $\phi$ be a class function on $G$ such that, for each elementary subgroup $H$ of $G$, we have $Res^{G}_{H}(\phi)\in B\otimes R(H)$. 
(Just consider $\phi=\sum_{H}\phi \cdot Ind_{H}^{G}f_{H}=\sum_{H}Ind_{H}^{G}(f_{H}\cdot Res_{H}^{G}\phi)$)

**Theorem**: Let $\phi$ be a calss function on $G$ such that, for each elementary subgroup $H$ of $G$, and each character $\chi$ of degree 1 of $H$, the number 
$$
\langle \chi,Res_{H}\phi \rangle_{H}=\frac{1}{\#H} \sum_{s \in H} \chi(s^{-1})\phi(s)
$$
belongs to $B$. Then $\phi$ belongs to $B\otimes R(G)$.
